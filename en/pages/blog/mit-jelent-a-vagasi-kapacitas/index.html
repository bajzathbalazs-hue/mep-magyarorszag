What does the right cutting capacity mean?

The term "cutting capacity" appears on every saw's data sheet, yet it's often misunderstood: it's not just a maximum size, but several interrelated parameters that are worth examining together.

What exactly does cutting capacity cover?

Cutting capacity indicates the material of a given cross-section (round, square, rectangular) that the machine can still safely cut, with the accuracy guaranteed by the manufacturer. This figure is always the combined result of the machine type and the built-in drive/guide system — it cannot be interpreted in isolation from the machine's other parameters.

Why isn't it enough to plan just for the "maximum" size?

Many buyers choose a machine based on the largest cross-section that occasionally occurs — this seems logical, but it hides two pitfalls:

  • Near its maximum capacity, a machine typically cuts more slowly and with greater blade/wheel wear than in the mid-range of its capacity — if most of the daily work operates at this limit, productivity suffers and operating costs rise.
  • Future needs (larger cross-section orders, a new product family) often only become apparent later — it's worth planning with a small reserve, if this fits within the investment budget.

The relationship between capacity and accuracy

Near the cutting capacity limit, cutting time typically increases and heat load rises, which can affect the quality of the cut surface and dimensional tolerance. If the cut piece goes directly into further production (e.g. turning, welding) without additional machining, it's advisable to choose a machine where the typical daily cross-section falls in the mid-range of the capacity, not at the upper limit.
